### A2. eps4 definition and grid-anisotropy correction (verbatim)

> "Lastly, we are able to resolve numerically very small anisotropies by incorporating quantitatively the contribution of the grid anisotropy. For a given value of $\epsilon_4$ used in $W({\bf n})$, we compute the equilibrium shape produced by the phase-field model. The grid-corrected anisotropy is then defined as that value of $\epsilon_4$ in $\gamma({\bf n})$ which produces a 3D equilibrium shape that matches exactly that of the phase-field model. A procedure that will be described elsewhere was also developed to obtain a grid-corrected $\tau({\bf n})$. Numerical tests were performed in 2D to check that this procedure yields accurate values of $\sigma^*$ over the entire range of $\epsilon_4$ investigated here in 3D."

The PRL never says outright that the Table I eps4 column holds the grid-corrected (effective) values. The context implies it, and the later Karma-group convention confirms it: Plapp and Karma 2000 report an "effective surface tension anisotropy $\epsilon_4^e$ which is slightly smaller than the 'bare' value", and quote $\epsilon_4^e = 0.025$ for bare $\epsilon_4 = 0.0284$ at $\Delta x = 0.8$ ($\tau_0 = 0.965$, kinetic anisotropy $\delta = 0.0364$). The kinetic correction ("grid-corrected $\tau({\bf n})$") is not specified in the PRL ("will be described elsewhere"). The KR98 abstract says the 3D simulations use "a methodology to calculate grid corrections due to the surface tension and kinetic anisotropies".

### A3. Numerical parameters stated in KR96 (verbatim)

> "Eqs. 5-6 were simulated explicitly with a grid spacing ranging between $0.6$ and $0.8$ with $W_0=1$, $D$ ranging between $0.5$ and $4$, a time step ranging between $0.01$ and $0.08$, and a constant undercooling $\Delta=0.45$. The anisotropy was varied from $\epsilon_4=0.0066$ to $\epsilon_4=0.047$ ..."

> "Several runs were performed to check that the values in Table 1 are independent of $d_0/W_0$ within an accuracy of 10%."

- dx/W0 = 0.6 to 0.8; dt = 0.01 to 0.08 (time unit not stated; tau0 is never given); D = 0.5 to 4 (in units of W0 and tau0).
- **lambda, d0/W0 and W0/d0 are not stated**, and neither is the d0/W0 range behind the 10% statement. Inference, not from the paper: if beta = 0 (lambda = D tau0/(a2 W0^2)) and tau0 = 1, then D = 0.5 to 4 maps to W0/d0 of about 0.9 to 7.2. tau0 is unstated and grid-corrected, so treat this only as an order of magnitude.
- Equations (verbatim forms): $f(\psi,u)=-\psi^2/2+\psi^4/4+\lambda u\psi(1-2\psi^2/3+\psi^4/5)$; $\tau({\bf n})\partial_t\psi=-\delta{\cal F}/\delta\psi$; $\partial_t u=D\nabla^2u+\frac12\partial_t\psi$; $\psi=+1$ solid, $-1$ liquid.
- Geometry: Table I runs on a 200x200x400 lattice, "long simulations that focused on the steady-state growth of a single dendrite tip along the $z$-axis". Fig. 1 is a separate 300x300x300 first-octant run at eps4 = 0.047.
- **Tip radius:** the PRL does not define how rho was measured. Secondary source: Karma, Lee and Plapp 2000 (arXiv cond-mat/9909021) say "For the phase-field shape, $\rho$ is computed from the $\phi=0^\circ$ section using the interpolation scheme that is described in Appendix B of Ref. [KarRap]". There [KarRap] = KR96 PRL + KR98 PRE, so Appendix B is in KR98. Plapp and Karma 2000: rho(t) "was calculated using the method described in Ref. [Karma98]" (= PRE 57, 4323).
- **Per-run V and rho:** not given. Only P and sigma* are tabulated (derived values above).
- Solvability columns (verbatim): numerical = "the standard axisymmetric approximation ... the same calculation performed by Kessler and Levine in Ref. [KesLev] [Acta Metall. 36, 2693 (1988)] and we have checked that our boundary integral code reproduces their results within one percent". Linear = "linear solvability theory of Barbieri and Langer which uses a shape linearized around the Ivantsov paraboloid of revolution and the same axisymmetric approximation".

## B. KR98 (item 2): PRE 57, 4323 (1998). Coordinator questions (a) to (d)

**Open full text: none found.** KR98 is not on arXiv: arXiv API author search `au:Karma AND au:Rappel` returns only patt-sol/9607005, chao-dyn/9608001 and cond-mat/9902017. The suggested IDs are wrong. **cond-mat/9711287** is Hinrichsen and Koduvely, "Numerical Study of Local and Global Persistence in Directed Percolation". **cond-mat/9710223** is M. Randeria, "Precursor Pairing Correlations and Pseudogaps". Semantic Scholar: openAccessPdf status CLOSED. OpenAlex: no OA location. A Scribd upload exists but is unofficial and was not used. So no statement can be quoted from KR98 itself. Below: the KR98 abstract plus open secondary texts by the same group that restate KR98's results.

- **(a) d0 = a1 W0/lambda, a1 = 0.8839 = 5 sqrt(2)/8.** SUPPORTED via secondary sources; the KR98 text itself was not checkable.
  - Karma 2001 (arXiv cond-mat/0103289, Eq. "kin"): "$d_0=a_1W/\lambda$ ... The expressions for the coefficients $a_1$ and $a_2$ are identical to those derived by KR [KarRap]".
  - Plapp and Karma 2000 (cond-mat/0002015): "$d_0 = a_1W_0/\lambda$ ... where $a_1 = 0.8839$ and $a_2 = 0.6267$ are numerical constants fixed by a solvability condition".
  - EFKP 2004 (cond-mat/0404164): "$a_1=5\sqrt{2}/8$ and $a_2=0.6267$ are the same numerical constants as in Ref. [KarRap]". Also: "$I=2\sqrt{2}/3$ and $J=16/15$, such that $a_1=5\sqrt{2}/8$".
  - Check: 5 sqrt(2)/8 = 0.88388.
- **(b) beta = a1[tau0/(lambda W0) - a2 W0/D], a2 = 0.6267, beta = 0 when tau0 = a2 lambda W0^2/D.** SUPPORTED via secondary sources.
  - Karma 2001: "$\beta=a_1\left[\tau/(W\lambda)-a_2W/D\right]$ ... interface kinetics can be eliminated ($\beta=0$) by choosing $\lambda=D\tau/(a_2W^2)$". This is algebraically identical to tau0 = a2 lambda W0^2/D.
  - Plapp and Karma 2000 give the anisotropic form $\beta(\hat n) = \frac{a_1}{\lambda}\frac{\tau(\hat n)}{W_0}\left(1-a_2 \lambda\frac{W(\hat n)^2}{D\tau(\hat n)}\right)$. They add: "for a $\tau(\hat n)$ as given by Eq. (anikin), the kinetic coefficient cannot be made to vanish simultaneously in all directions, but for small anisotropies choosing $\delta_4=2\epsilon_4$ is a sufficiently accurate approximation."
- **(c) Order of the remaining error.** PARTLY, and no KR98 quote exists to support "O(p^2)".
  - EFKP 2004: "Karma and Rappel remarked that the physically relevant length scales for the outer problem are not the capillary length, but rather the diffusion length $D/V$ or a local radius of curvature $\rho$. Calculations performed with the expansion parameter $p=WV/D$ for the symmetric model of solidification ($D_s=D_l$, or $q(\phi)=1$) yield, to first order in $p$, a new expression for the interface kinetic coefficient that contains a finite-interface thickness correction. ... It was also shown that this correction can be obtained in a second-order expansion in $\epsilon$ [KarRap, Alm]."
  - Almgren 1999 abstract: "second-order 'classical' asymptotics".
  - Supported: the analysis is carried to first order in p for the symmetric model. **Not shown by any text I could read:** that "the remaining error is O(p^2)". Safe wording: "the thin-interface asymptotics of KR98 are carried out to first order in the interface Péclet number p = W0 V/D [KR98; see also EFKP04, Almgren99]". Do not attribute an explicit O(p^2) error statement to KR98 unless you check the PRE text yourself.
- **(d) lambda or W0/d0 and dx/W0 for the 3D runs.** KR96 gives dx = 0.6 to 0.8 W0, D = 0.5 to 4 and dt = 0.01 to 0.08, but **no lambda or W0/d0** (Section A3). KR98: UNCHECKABLE (no open text).
  - Karma, Lee and Plapp 2000 say their 3D phase-field region used "the same choice of computational parameters as in Ref. [KarRap]", without listing them.
  - The nearest openly documented Karma-group 3D parameter set is Plapp and Karma 2000 at Delta = 0.1: "$W_0=1$, $\Delta x=0.8$, $\epsilon_4=0.0284$, $\tau_0=0.965$, $\delta=0.0364$, ... $D=24$, $\Delta t=0.004$, and $\lambda=39.6$ (giving $d_0 = 0.0223$, $\beta_0=0$)". This is not KR98's Delta = 0.45 set.
- KR98 abstract on the 3D result (verbatim): "For small crystalline anisotropy, simulated values of $\sigma^*$ are slightly larger than solvability theory predictions computed by the boundary integral method assuming an axisymmetric shape, and agree relatively well with experiments for succinonitrile given the uncertainty in the measured anisotropy. In contrast, for large anisotropy, simulated $\sigma^*$ values are significantly larger than the predicted values."
